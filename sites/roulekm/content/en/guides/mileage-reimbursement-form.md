**There's no single official mileage reimbursement form: your employer sets its own.** What the IRS sets is what the
form has to show if your employer runs an accountable plan, the kind of plan that keeps the money out of your taxable
wages. For **each business trip: the date, where you went, the business purpose and the miles**, written down at or
near the time and handed in within a reasonable period. The IRS rules treat accounting **within 60 days after the
trip** as reasonable in every case, or **within 120 days of a statement your employer sends at least every quarter**.
Check your employer's policy for its own deadline.

The IRS rules accept any account book, diary, log, statement of expense, trip sheet or similar record, and Publication
463 says your employer should tell you how it reimburses and which records to provide. Its sample mileage log is
marked "not an official Internal Revenue form". An official form you may come across, Optional Form 1164 from
the U.S. General Services Administration, is built for federal agencies: it asks for the department or bureau and a
voucher number.

## The lines a mileage reimbursement form usually has

If your employer gives you a form, use it. If it asks you to make your own, or you want to check yours, these are the
usual columns. Only the first four are what the IRS asks for each trip (Publication 463, Table 5-1, and Rev. Proc.
2019-46); the rest are common practice.

| Column | What goes in it | Asked by the IRS? |
| ------ | --------------- | ----------------- |
| **Date** | The day of the trip | Yes |
| **To** (destination) | The business place you drove to | Yes |
| **Business purpose** | Why the trip was for work: the client, the site, the meeting | Yes |
| **Miles** | The business miles for that trip | Yes |
| From | Where you started (the office, a client) | Common practice |
| Rate | Your employer's rate per mile | Common practice |
| Amount | Miles × rate for the trip | Common practice |
| Odometer start and end | The car's readings | Common practice (Publication 463's sample log has them) |
| Tolls, parking | Business tolls and parking, with receipts | Common practice, see below |

And once per form: your name and department, the period it covers, the total, your signature (often a line certifying
that the claim is true), and the approver's signature. Optional Form 1164 has the same pieces: date, from, to, number
of miles, the mileage amount and a column for fares or tolls, then the total, an approval line and the claimant's
certification.

A few rules for filling in the lines:

- **A round trip can be one line**, and so can one outing with several business stops; a stop for lunch between two
  of them doesn't break it.
- **Commuting doesn't go on the form**: driving between home and your main or regular workplace is personal, not
  business. Our guide to [IRS mileage log requirements](/en/guides/irs-mileage-log-requirements/) sets out what counts
  as business miles.
- **Copy the form from your log.** Publication 463 says an expense statement you give your employer can count as a
  timely record when it's copied from your log, and a log kept up each week is timely. It adds that a record made at
  the time carries more weight than a statement prepared later.

## Adding it up

**Miles × your employer's rate per mile.** Federal tax rules don't make your employer use the IRS rate: Publication
15-B says an employer *may* use it to reimburse business use of a personal car, and Rev. Proc. 2019-46 lets a mileage
allowance be paid at the IRS rate, at a flat rate, per mile or on a schedule. Your employer's policy says which rate
goes on your form.

**The IRS business rate for 2026** depends on the date: **72.5 cents a mile** for trips before July 1, 2026 (Notice
2026-10), and **76 cents a mile** for trips on or after July 1, 2026 reimbursed on or after that date (Announcement
2026-11). A form that covers trips on both sides of July 1 therefore has two IRS rates. These are the rates the IRS
published, checked on November 24, 2026; the 2027 rate wasn't out yet.

**Example, a month on one form** (fictional figures, to show the method). An employee with a regular office drives
their own car for these trips in October 2026:

| Date | From | To | Business purpose | Miles |
| ---- | ---- | -- | ---------------- | ----: |
| Thu Oct 1, 2026 | Office, 400 Elm St | Harbor Supply, 18 Dock Rd, and back | Deliver samples | 24 |
| Thu Oct 8, 2026 | Office | 77 Ridge Ave, and back | Installation check (Lopez) | 36 |
| Wed Oct 21, 2026 | Office | 3 Mill Ln, then 12 Bay St, back to the office | Two client visits (Chen, Ortiz) | 47 |
| Wed Oct 28, 2026 | Office | Regional office, 950 River Rd, and back | Quarterly sales meeting | 33 |
| | | | **Total** | **140** |

Reimbursed in November, at the IRS rate for those dates, 140 × $0.76 = **$106.40**. If the employer's rate is 65 cents a mile, the same month
comes to 140 × $0.65 = **$91.00**. Under the IRS's 60-day safe harbor, counted from each trip, handing this form in by
November 30, 2026 keeps every trip on it within the period, October 1 being the first.

**Paid more than the IRS rate, or a flat car allowance instead?** That changes how the money is taxed, not what goes on
the form: the part above the IRS rate is taxable pay, and so is an allowance you don't have to account for. Our guide
on [whether mileage reimbursement is taxable](/en/guides/is-mileage-reimbursement-taxable/) explains both.
**Paid less?** Most employees can't deduct the difference on their own return: see the
[mileage tax deduction](/en/guides/mileage-tax-deduction/) and its exceptions.

## What to attach, and what to keep

- **The trip log behind the form**, if your employer asks for it. Publication 463 says you account to your employer
  with the record of each trip along with documentary evidence, and that you give your employer the same type of
  records the IRS would ask for.
- **Tolls and parking on their own lines.** The IRS standard rate stands in for the cost of running and owning the car
  (gas, oil, repairs, tires, insurance, depreciation or lease payments, registration). In the IRS's rules for the
  standard rate, business tolls and parking fees come on top of it as separate items, and forms such as Optional Form
  1164 give them their own column. Parking at your regular workplace is commuting. Whether your employer pays tolls
  and parking is its policy.
- **Receipts.** Publication 463 generally asks for receipts, but not for an expense (other than lodging) under $75, or
  a transportation expense with no receipt readily available. Your employer may ask for every receipt anyway.
- **Copies.** Publication 463 says employees who give their records to their employer and are reimbursed generally
  don't have to keep copies, except in cases it lists: claiming deductions above the reimbursement, a nonaccountable
  plan, an employer that doesn't use adequate accounting procedures to verify expense accounts, or an employer you're
  related to.

## What to check with your employer, the IRS or a tax professional

- **Your employer's policy and form first**: the rate per mile, the period each form covers, its deadline, what to
  attach, whether it pays tolls and parking, and who approves. Publication 463 says your employer should tell you the
  method it uses and the records you must provide.
- **The rate for your trips' dates**, on the IRS's Standard mileage rates page, if your employer uses the IRS rate.
- **How the reimbursement is taxed**, if it's above the IRS rate or a flat allowance: your W-2 and our guide on
  [mileage reimbursement and taxes](/en/guides/is-mileage-reimbursement-taxable/).

Publications 463 and 15 on irs.gov explain the rules. For your own situation, ask your employer's payroll or expenses
team, a tax professional or the IRS.
