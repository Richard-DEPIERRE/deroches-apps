This is the **US federal (IRS) rule**, for tax year 2026. **If you're self-employed**, you can deduct the business use
of your car on **Schedule C (Form 1040)**. That includes sole proprietors, independent contractors and gig drivers who
report their 1099 income there. You figure the deduction with either the **standard mileage rate** (72.5 cents a mile
for business miles driven from January 1 to June 30, 2026, and 76 cents from July 1) or your **actual car expenses**,
under conditions the IRS sets. **If you're an employee paid on a W-2, you generally can't deduct unreimbursed
mileage.** That rule has applied since 2018, and a federal law of July 4, 2025 made it permanent. The IRS names a few
exceptions, listed below.

## Who can deduct business mileage on a federal return

**The self-employed.** The IRS's Topic no. 510 lists being self-employed among the cases where car expenses can be
deducted. Publication 463 says a sole proprietor reports income and expenses on Schedule C (a farmer on Schedule F) and
doesn't use Form 2106. The Schedule C instructions add that Schedule C is also where certain amounts shown on a Form
1099 (1099-NEC, 1099-MISC or 1099-K) are reported, and the IRS counts driving for rides or deliveries booked through an
app as gig work.

**Statutory employees.** If the "Statutory employee" box (box 13) is checked on your W-2, the Schedule C instructions say
to report that income and its expenses on Schedule C. They name full-time life insurance agents, certain agent or
commission drivers and traveling salespersons, and certain homeworkers.

**Employees paid on a W-2.** Publication 463 says the cost of using your car as an employee, at the standard rate or at
actual cost, can't be claimed as a miscellaneous itemized deduction for tax years beginning after 2017. Public Law
119-21 of July 4, 2025 (section 70110) removed the end date of that rule, and IRS Notice 2026-10 says it "made permanent
the disallowance" of these deductions, "including unreimbursed employee travel expenses". So for 2026, an employee's
unreimbursed mileage generally isn't deductible.

**The exceptions the IRS names.** Expenses deducted in figuring adjusted gross income are not affected. The Form 2106
instructions (2025) say the form may be used only by:

- Armed Forces reservists;
- qualified performing artists;
- fee-basis state or local government officials;
- employees with impairment-related work expenses.

Notice 2026-10 also names eligible educators, who can deduct certain unreimbursed employee travel expenses under their
own rules. Each group has its own definition and conditions, set out in the Form 2106 instructions, Publication 463
and Notice 2026-10.

**If your employer reimburses you.** That goes through your employer's accountable plan, not your deduction. When the plan's
three rules are met (a business connection, an adequate accounting within a reasonable time, and any excess returned),
Publication 463 says the reimbursement shouldn't be included in your W-2 wages. Your employer can tell you how its plan works,
and our guide on [whether mileage reimbursement is taxable](/en/guides/is-mileage-reimbursement-taxable/) covers the
plan rules, payments above the IRS rate and flat car allowances.

## Two ways to figure it: the standard mileage rate or actual expenses

**The standard mileage rate.** Multiply your business miles by the IRS rate for the period you drove them. Business
parking fees and tolls can be added on top; parking at your place of work is commuting and doesn't count. If you use
the standard rate for a year, you can't also deduct your actual car costs for that year: depreciation, lease payments,
maintenance and repairs, gas, oil, insurance or registration fees. For these rules a "car" includes a van, pickup or
panel truck, and the IRS says its rates apply to fully electric and hybrid cars as well as gas and diesel ones.

**Actual expenses.** You add up what the car actually cost to run: gas, oil, repairs, tires, insurance, registration
fees, licenses and depreciation or lease payments (Publication 463 also lists garage rent, tolls and parking fees).
If the car is used for both business and personal driving, only the business share counts, and you can split it by
miles. In Publication 463's example, a contractor drives 20,000 miles in the year, 12,000 of them for business, so 60%
of the cost of running the car is a business expense. Depreciation has its own limits and rules, in chapter 4 of
Publication 463.

Publication 463 notes that if you qualify for both methods, you may want to figure the deduction both ways.

## The IRS conditions on using the standard mileage rate

**Choosing it.**

- **A car you own:** you choose the standard rate in the **first year the car is available for use in your business**.
  In later years, you can choose either the standard rate or actual expenses.
- **A car you lease:** if you choose the standard rate, you use it **for the entire lease period, including renewals**.
- You make the choice by the due date of your return, including extensions, and can't revoke it. If you later switch
  to actual expenses before the car is fully depreciated, Publication 463 says you use straight-line depreciation over
  the car's remaining estimated useful life.

**When you can't use it.** Publication 463 and Topic no. 510 rule out the standard rate if you:

- use **five or more cars at the same time**, as in a fleet operation (cars you use at different times don't count as
  at the same time);
- claimed depreciation on the car with any method other than straight line for its estimated useful life;
- used the Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System (MACRS);
- claimed a section 179 deduction on the car;
- claimed the special depreciation allowance on the car;
- claimed actual car expenses after 1997 for a car you lease.

Publication 334 adds a rural mail carrier who received a qualified reimbursement. A car for hire, such as a taxi, can
use the standard rate unless one of these conditions applies. An employee using a vehicle provided by the employer
can't use it.

## The 2026 rate

| Business miles driven | IRS business standard mileage rate | Source |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------ |
| Jan. 1 – June 30, 2026 | 72.5 cents a mile | Notice 2026-10 |
| July 1 – Dec. 31, 2026 | 76 cents a mile | Announcement 2026-11 |

These are the rates as the IRS published them, checked on November 19, 2026. The date of each trip decides its rate:
our guide to [IRS mileage log requirements](/en/guides/irs-mileage-log-requirements/) works through an example with
both halves of 2026. For driving in 2025 the rate was 70 cents a mile. The IRS hadn't published the 2027 rate when we
checked (the 2026 rate was announced on December 29, 2025): look for it on the IRS's Standard mileage rates page.

The IRS also sets separate rates for medical, charitable and, for certain moves such as active-duty military ones,
moving miles. Those are different deductions with their own rules, listed on the same IRS page.

If you use the standard rate, Roulekm (coming soon to iPhone and Android) applies the IRS business rate to each trip by
its date, 72.5 cents before July 1, 2026 and 76 cents from then on, and keeps the year's total. It doesn't work out
actual expenses.

## Where it goes on your return

For the self-employed, on **Schedule C (Form 1040)**. The line numbers below are those of the 2026 Schedule C form and of
the 2025 instructions, the latest on irs.gov on November 19, 2026:

- **Line 9, Car and truck expenses.** At the standard rate, the instructions have you multiply your business miles by
  the rate, add business parking fees and tolls, and enter the total on line 9. With actual expenses, the business part
  of gas, oil, repairs, insurance, license plates and so on goes on line 9; depreciation goes on line 13, and rent or
  lease payments on line 20a.
- **Part IV, Information on Your Vehicle (lines 43 to 47).** You complete it if you claim the standard rate, lease the
  vehicle, or it's fully depreciated, and you don't have to file Form 4562 for another reason. It asks when the vehicle
  was placed in service, its business, commuting and other miles for the year, whether it was available for personal
  use, and whether you have evidence for the deduction and whether it's written. With more than one vehicle, you attach
  a statement with the same information for each additional one.
- **Form 4562, Part V** takes the place of Part IV if you claim depreciation on the vehicle, or must file Form 4562 for
  any other reason.

A farmer reports car expenses on Schedule F, line 10, with Form 4562, Part V. An employee in one of the exceptions above
uses Form 2106, whose instructions say where the result goes. For your own return, check the instructions for the year
you file.

## Keep a log

Topic no. 510 says the law requires you to back your expenses with adequate records or sufficient evidence, and Part IV
asks whether your evidence is written. What each entry needs, what counts as business miles (commuting doesn't) and how long to keep it are in our guide to
[IRS mileage log requirements](/en/guides/irs-mileage-log-requirements/). Driving for work in the UK? HMRC has its own
rules: see our guide to the [self-employed mileage claim, the UK rule](/en/guides/self-employed-mileage-claim/).

## What to check with the IRS or a tax professional

This guide explains the federal rules. A few points depend on your own situation:

- **Whether you're self-employed for this work or an employee**, and whether your W-2 marks you as a statutory employee.
- **If you're in one of the employee exceptions**, its definition and conditions, in the Form 2106 instructions.
- **Which method your car can use**, given its history: the first year you used it for business, a lease, depreciation
  already claimed.
- **Your forms and lines** for the year you file, and anything beyond the federal return, which this guide doesn't cover.

Publication 463, Topic no. 510 and the Schedule C instructions on irs.gov explain the rules. For your own return, ask a
tax professional or the IRS.
