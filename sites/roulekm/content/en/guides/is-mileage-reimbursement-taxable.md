**Not if your employer reimburses you under an accountable plan.** The IRS rule is that money paid back under an
accountable plan isn't wages: it stays out of box 1 of your W-2 and isn't subject to income tax withholding, social
security or Medicare tax. A plan is accountable when it has three rules: the miles are for your employer's business,
you account for them within a reasonable time, and you return anything you were paid beyond what you accounted for.
**Under a nonaccountable plan, such as a flat car allowance with nothing to account for, it's taxable pay**: your employer
adds it to your wages in box 1, with withholding and payroll taxes. And if a per-mile rate is higher than the IRS
standard mileage rate, the part above that rate is taxable too.

This is the federal tax rule, from IRS Publications 463 and 15. Your employer decides which kind of plan it runs.

## The accountable plan: three rules

Publication 463 (for employees) and Publication 15 (for employers) set out the same three rules. A reimbursement counts
as paid under an accountable plan only if:

1. **It's for business.** You paid the cost while working for your employer, and it isn't money that would otherwise
   have been your pay. Driving between home and your regular workplace is personal commuting, not business, and
   Publication 463 treats a reimbursement for a non-deductible expense as paid under a nonaccountable plan.
2. **You account for it within a reasonable period.** For miles, that means a record made at or near the time showing
   the date, the place and the business purpose of each trip, and the miles. What that record needs is in our guide to
   [IRS mileage log requirements](/en/guides/irs-mileage-log-requirements/).
3. **You return any excess within a reasonable period**, such as an advance for miles you didn't drive.

What counts as reasonable depends on the facts, but both publications accept these times: an advance within 30 days of
the expense, accounting within 60 days after it, and any excess returned within 120 days; or, if your employer sends a
statement at least every quarter, accounting or returning within 120 days of it.

**Paid under an accountable plan**, the reimbursement isn't wages. Publication 463 says your employer shouldn't include
it in box 1 of your W-2, and if it did although all three rules were met, to ask your employer for a corrected W-2.

**Paid under a nonaccountable plan**, the money is wages. Publication 15 lists the cases: you don't have to account for
the miles (or don't), you don't have to return what you didn't spend (or don't), the money is paid whether or not you
have business expenses, or it would otherwise have been your pay. Your employer adds it to box 1 with your salary and
treats it as supplemental wages. Publication 463 adds that you can't turn a nonaccountable plan into an accountable one
by keeping records and returning money of your own accord.

## Paid per mile: up to the IRS rate, and above it

When your employer pays a rate per mile, the IRS treats the amount as accounted for when it isn't more than the federal
rate, which for a car is the IRS standard mileage rate; you still have to show your employer the miles, the
dates and the business purpose. Under Rev. Proc. 2019-46, the amount treated as accounted for is the lower of what you
were paid or **the standard mileage rate times the business miles you accounted for**.

- **At or below the IRS rate**, the reimbursement stays off box 1 of your W-2.
- **Above the IRS rate**, the employer reports the part up to the rate in **box 12 with code L**, where it isn't taxed,
  and the part above it as wages in boxes 1, 3 and 5, with income tax withholding, social security and Medicare tax.
  Rev. Proc. 2019-46 doesn't require the plan to take back that extra for miles you accounted for: it's taxed instead.

**The rate for 2026 depends on the dates.** The IRS business rate is **72.5 cents a mile** (Notice 2026-10) for mileage
paid to you before July 1, 2026, or for driving before that date, and **76 cents a mile** when both the driving and the
payment are on or after July 1, 2026: Announcement 2026-11 raised the rate and set these dates. Those are the rates the IRS published, checked on
November 21, 2026; the 2027 rate wasn't out yet.

**Example, using the IRS's own method.** You drive 1,000 business miles in your own car between August and October 2026
and record each trip. In November 2026 your employer's accountable plan pays you 80 cents a mile, $800. The IRS rate for
those miles is 76 cents, so $760 (1,000 × $0.76) is treated as accounted for: it goes in box 12 with code L and isn't
taxed. The other $40 is wages in box 1. Publication 463 works through a similar case for 2025, at 75 cents a mile
against that year's 70.

**Paid less than the IRS rate?** Under an accountable plan the whole amount stays out of your wages, but most employees
can't deduct the difference on their own return. That rule, and the few exceptions, are in our guide to the
[mileage tax deduction](/en/guides/mileage-tax-deduction/).

## A flat car allowance, or a FAVR plan

**A flat monthly allowance.** Publication 463 gives the example of $1,000 a month for business expenses with no proof
asked and no need to return what isn't spent: the employer includes the full $12,000 on the W-2 as wages. A fixed
allowance can still be part of an accountable plan: Rev. Proc. 2019-46 says that for an allowance not paid only per
mile, the return rule is met if you must give back the part above the standard mileage rate times the business miles
you accounted for. You still have to account for the miles.

**A fixed and variable rate (FAVR) plan.** This combines a flat periodic amount for the car's fixed costs (depreciation
or lease payments, insurance and the like) with a cents-per-mile amount for running costs such as gas and oil, worked
out from costs where you're based. Rev. Proc. 2019-46 sets its conditions, among them:

- you account for at least 5,000 business miles in the year (or 80% of the plan's annual business mileage, if more);
- the plan covers at least five employees at all times, and most of them aren't managers;
- the car is one you own or lease, and within 30 days of the plan first covering it you give your employer its details
  in writing (make, model and year, insurance, odometer reading, price).

Paid under these rules, a FAVR allowance is treated as accounted for (less anything you had to return and didn't), as
long as you report the dates, places and business purpose of your trips; any part above the amount the IRS treats as
accounted for is taxable. If your employer
uses FAVR, Publication 463 says it will ask you for the records it needs.

## Do employers have to reimburse mileage?

The IRS rules above decide how a reimbursement is taxed, not whether your employer pays one. Publication 463 says the
employer decides whether to reimburse under an accountable or a nonaccountable plan, and describes employees paid a
salary or commission on the understanding that they pay their own expenses, who have no reimbursement arrangement.

**Federal wage law.** The Fair Labor Standards Act regulations say wages must be paid "free and clear" (29 CFR 531.35).
The regulation's example is tools of the trade an employer requires the employee to provide: there is a violation in any
workweek when their cost cuts into the minimum or overtime wages the law requires. It doesn't name cars or set a mileage
rate. These are the U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division regulations: it can say how they apply to a job.

**State law.** Some states have their own rules. In California, the Labor Commissioner's Office says an employee is
entitled to be reimbursed for all expenses or losses incurred in direct consequence of carrying out their work duties
(Labor Code section 2802). For
any other state, check with its labor department.

This is general information on what the rules say. Whether your employer owes you a reimbursement is a question for
your state's labor department, the Wage and Hour Division or an employment lawyer.

## What to check with your employer, the IRS or a tax professional

- **Your employer's plan**: whether it's accountable, the rate per mile, what you have to hand in (the log, the business
  purpose) and its deadlines for accounting and for returning advances. Publication 463 says to ask your employer if
  you aren't sure which kind of plan it is. The usual lines and a worked month are in our guide to the
  [mileage reimbursement form](/en/guides/mileage-reimbursement-form/).
- **Your W-2**: reimbursements under an accountable plan shouldn't be in box 1. If a per-mile allowance was above the
  IRS rate, box 12 with code L shows the part up to the rate, which isn't taxed, and the extra is in box 1.
- **Special cases**: for a car allowance, if you own more than 10% of the company's stock or your employer is a close
  relative (Publication 463 lists who counts), you must be able to prove your expenses to the IRS even if you accounted
  to your employer.
- **The rate for the dates of your trips**, on the IRS's Standard mileage rates page.

Publications 463 and 15 and the Form W-2 instructions on irs.gov explain the rules. For your own situation, ask your
employer's payroll team, a tax professional or the IRS.
