This is the **US federal (IRS) rule**. To deduct business driving, the IRS asks you to prove each business use of
the car, and the same records back a reimbursement under an employer's accountable plan. For every business use, the
log shows **the date, your business destination, the business purpose and the miles driven**. For the year, it also
shows **the total miles you drove the car**, business and personal. The IRS asks for a record made **at or near the
time of the trip** (a log kept up each week counts), and you generally keep it **3 years** after you file, longer in
some cases. The details, a worked example and what counts as commuting are below.

## What each entry needs to show

IRS Publication 463 (2025 edition, for 2025 returns) sums up the records for car use in its Table 5-1. The regulation
behind it, 26 CFR 1.274-5T, lists the same elements:

| What to prove | What the log records |
| ------------- | -------------------- |
| **Time** | The date of each business use of the car |
| **Place** | Your business destination |
| **Business purpose** | Why the trip was for business (the client, the job site, the meeting) |
| **Amount** | The miles for each business use, and the total miles for the year |

For car expenses, the same table also lists the cost of the car and any improvements, and the date you started using
it for business.

A few rules from the same chapter of Publication 463:

- **No estimates.** The publication says you can't deduct amounts that you approximate or estimate, and the regulation
  rules out deductions based on approximations or unsupported testimony.
- **Any written record, no official form.** An account book, diary, log, trip sheets or a similar record works, and a
  record kept on a computer counts. The regulation says the same of a logging program. The sample log in Publication
  463 (Table 5-2: date, destination, business purpose, odometer at start and stop, miles for the trip) is marked
  "not an official Internal Revenue form".
- **One entry per trip is enough.** A round trip, or several stops in one uninterrupted business use, can be a single
  record. A stop for lunch between two business stops doesn't break it.
- **Routes you drive again and again.** If you drive the same route (a sales or delivery route), the IRS example has
  you record the route's length once, the date of each trip at or near the time, and the car's total miles for the
  year.
- **Confidential details.** A place, business purpose or business relationship that is confidential may be recorded
  elsewhere instead of in the log, as long as you write it down at or near the time and can show it.

## When to write it: "timely", not necessarily the same day

People often search for a "contemporaneous mileage log". Here is what the IRS texts actually say:

- Publication 463 says to record each business use **at or near the time** of the use. You don't need to write it
  down the same day: **a log kept on a weekly basis that accounts for use during the week** is considered a timely
  kept record.
- The regulation says in so many words that **"a contemporaneous log is not required"**. But a record made at or near
  the time, supported by documentary evidence, has a "high degree of credibility". A statement prepared later needs
  corroborating evidence of "a high degree of probative value" to reach that level.
- **Part of the year.** An adequate record kept for part of the year can prove the business use for the whole year,
  if other evidence shows that part is representative. Publication 463's example is a log kept the first week of
  each month, with invoices showing the same pattern in the other weeks.

In practice, a log written after each trip, or at the end of each week, is the kind of record the IRS describes. A
log rebuilt from memory at tax time needs strong supporting evidence to carry the same weight.

## What counts as business miles, and what doesn't

Publication 463, chapter 4, and the revenue ruling it rests on (Rev. Rul. 99-7) draw the line like this.

**Business miles** include driving:

- from one workplace to another, within the area of your tax home;
- to visit clients or customers;
- to a business meeting away from your regular workplace;
- from home to a temporary workplace, when you have one or more regular places of work.

**Commuting is not business.** Driving between home and your main or regular place of work is a personal commuting
expense. Publication 463 says it isn't deductible **no matter how far** you live, and **even if you work during the
trip**: business calls on the way, or a colleague riding along to talk business, don't change that. Neither does an
advertising display on the car, or hauling your tools (only the extra cost, such as renting a trailer, can be
deducted).

The publication's special cases:

| Your trip | As the IRS writes it |
| --------- | -------------------- |
| Home → main or regular job | Commuting, never deductible |
| Home → temporary work location, and you have a regular job elsewhere | Business, whatever the distance |
| Home → temporary work site, and you have no regular place of work but ordinarily work in the metropolitan area where you live | Business only if the site is outside that metropolitan area; inside it, commuting |
| Home office that is your principal place of business → another work location in the same business | Business |
| Regular job → second job, the same day | Business (only the direct route counts) |
| Home → part-time job on a day off from your main job | Commuting |
| No regular office and no home office | Home → first business contact and last contact → home are commuting; client to client is business |

A work location is generally **temporary** if the work there is realistically expected to last (and does in fact
last) **one year or less**. In the log, a commute you don't count as business still counts in the car's total miles
for the year.

## How long to keep the log

The IRS page "How long should I keep records?" says to keep the records that support a deduction until the period of
limitations for that return runs out:

- **3 years** in the general case, counted from the date you filed (a return filed before the due date counts as
  filed on the due date);
- **longer in some cases** listed on that page: 3 years from filing or 2 years from paying the tax, whichever is
  later, if you claim a credit or refund; 6 years if you leave out income worth more than 25% of the gross income on
  your return; 7 years for a loss from worthless securities or a bad debt deduction; indefinitely if you don't file
  or file a fraudulent return.

Publication 463 adds that **records of the car's business use are kept for each year of its recovery period** (its
depreciation). An employee who gives the records to the employer and is reimbursed generally doesn't need to keep
copies, except in the cases the publication lists (for example, claiming more than the reimbursement, or a
nonaccountable plan).

## Example: a week of entries

A fictional example, to show the IRS method. A self-employed contractor has a workshop she drives to every day and
one car, used for business and personal driving. Her odometer read 41,250 on January 1, 2026.

| Date | From → destination | Business purpose | Miles |
| ---- | ------------------ | ---------------- | ----: |
| Mon Nov 2, 2026 | Workshop → 14 Oak Ave → workshop | Estimate, bathroom remodel (Patel) | 22 |
| Tue Nov 3, 2026 | Workshop → 14 Oak Ave → 9 Lake Rd → workshop | Job site, then estimate (Nguyen) | 27 |
| Wed Nov 4, 2026 | Workshop → 14 Oak Ave → workshop | Job site | 18 |
| Thu Nov 5, 2026 | Workshop → 220 Pine St → workshop | Client meeting, contract (Nguyen) | 12 |
| Fri Nov 6, 2026 | Workshop → 14 Oak Ave → workshop | Job site | 18 |

Her drives from home to the workshop and back are commuting, so they aren't in the business log. They do show in the
car's total. On December 31 her odometer reads 53,870, so the car's **total for 2026 is 12,620 miles**, of which her
log adds up to **3,480 business miles**: 1,700 from January to June and 1,780 from July to December. Business miles
divided by total miles gives the car's business-use percentage (3,480 ÷ 12,620 = 27.6%), the calculation Publication
463 uses when a car is used for more than one purpose.

### Why the date matters for 2026

If the standard mileage rate is used, the date also decides the rate, because 2026 has two:

| Business miles driven | IRS business standard mileage rate | Source |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------ |
| Jan. 1 – June 30, 2026 | 72.5 cents a mile | Notice 2026-10 (IR-2025-128) |
| July 1 – Dec. 31, 2026 | 76 cents a mile | Announcement 2026-11 |

In the example: 1,700 × $0.725 = $1,232.50, plus 1,780 × $0.76 = $1,352.80, which comes to **$2,585.30** for 2026 at
the standard rate. Business-related parking fees and tolls can be deducted in addition to the standard rate; parking
at your place of work is commuting. For comparison, the 2025 rate was 70 cents a mile. These are the 2026 rates as
the IRS published them, checked on November 11, 2026; for driving in 2027, check the IRS standard mileage rates page.

This example shows the method only. It doesn't say whether the standard rate or actual expenses fits a given car. The
IRS sets conditions for using the standard rate (Publication 463, chapter 4).

## What to check with the IRS or a tax professional

This guide explains what a log should contain. It doesn't say whether you can deduct car expenses, or how. A few
points depend on your own situation:

- **Who can deduct.** The IRS's Topic no. 510 says most employees cannot deduct unreimbursed car expenses, with
  exceptions for members of the Armed Forces or military reservists, qualified performing artists, fee-basis state or
  local government officials, and the self-employed.
- **A reimbursement from your employer.** The same records back an expense report under an accountable plan. Ask your
  employer which records and rate it uses.
- **Your figures and forms.** Publication 463 and Topic no. 510 on irs.gov explain the methods, conditions and forms.
  For your own return, ask a tax professional or the IRS.
