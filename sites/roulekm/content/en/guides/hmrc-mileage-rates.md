For the **2026 to 2027 tax year** (6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027), HMRC's approved mileage rates are **55p a mile for
the first 10,000 business miles by car or van, then 25p a mile**. Motorcycles are 24p a mile and bicycles 20p a mile,
for every business mile. The rate for the first 10,000 car and van miles was 45p before 6 April 2026.

| Vehicle | 2026 to 2027 tax year (from 6 April 2026) | 2011 to 2012 up to 2025 to 2026 |
| ------- | ----------------------------------------: | ------------------------------: |
| Car or van, first 10,000 business miles in the tax year | 55p a mile | 45p a mile |
| Car or van, each business mile over 10,000 | 25p a mile | 25p a mile |
| Motorcycle | 24p a mile | 24p a mile |
| Bicycle | 20p a mile | 20p a mile |
| Passenger in a car or van (per fellow employee carried) | 5p a mile | 5p a mile |

- The 55p is in section 230 of the Income Tax (Earnings and Pensions) Act 2003. Section 2 of the Taxation (Energy and
  Vehicles) Act 2026 set it, for the 2026 to 2027 tax year and later years.
- An electrically assisted pedal bike uses the bicycle rate. Any other electric bike uses the motorcycle rate.
- Electric and hybrid cars and vans that you own use the car and van rate. Company cars are different (see below).

These are the rates on HMRC's "Travel — mileage and fuel rates and allowances" page and in the Act, checked on 27
November 2026. Changes for 2027 to 2028 have been announced but are not law yet: see the last section.

## Which rate applies to you

The same pence per mile are used in several ways, depending on who is paying and whose vehicle it is.

| Your situation | What the rate is used for | More detail |
| -------------- | ------------------------- | ----------- |
| Employee, own vehicle, employer pays per mile | The most the employer can pay without tax | below |
| Employee, own vehicle, paid less than that or nothing | Tax relief on the difference | [mileage allowance relief](/en/guides/mileage-allowance-relief/) |
| Self-employed, simplified expenses | A flat rate per business mile | [self-employed mileage claim](/en/guides/self-employed-mileage-claim/) |
| Employer working out National Insurance | 55p for every car or van mile, no 10,000-mile band | below |
| Carrying a colleague on a business journey | 5p a mile per passenger | below |
| Company car | Not these rates: HMRC's advisory fuel rates | below |

### Employers paying for your own vehicle

What an employer pays you for business journeys in your own car, van, motorcycle or bicycle is a **mileage allowance
payment**. Up to the **approved amount** (your business miles in the tax year × the rate for that kind of vehicle),
there is no tax on it and nothing for the employer to report to HMRC. If it pays more, gov.uk says the employer either
reports the excess on form P11D, or adds it to your pay and takes tax off as normal.

If your employer pays less than the approved amount, or nothing, you can get **mileage allowance relief** on the
difference. How it's worked out and claimed is in the guide to
[mileage allowance relief for employees](/en/guides/mileage-allowance-relief/).

### The self-employed

Sole traders, and business partnerships with no company as a partner, who use **simplified expenses** claim the same pence per mile as a flat
rate for cars, goods vehicles and motorcycles: 55p then 25p, and 24p, for 2026 to 2027. There is no bicycle rate under
simplified expenses. Who can use it, and the rules on choosing it, are in the guide to the
[self-employed mileage claim](/en/guides/self-employed-mileage-claim/).

### National Insurance

National Insurance doesn't use the 10,000-mile band. For mileage payments for an employee's own vehicle, gov.uk's
employer guide gives one rate for **every business mile**: **55p for cars and vans from 6 April 2026** (45p before),
24p for motorcycles and 20p for bicycles. It is worked out on the miles in each pay period, not over the tax year (the
Social Security (Contributions) Regulations 2001, regulation 22A). Anything paid above that is added to the employee's
earnings for Class 1 National Insurance in that pay period; the tax side is worked out separately, over the tax year, as
above. There is no relief for National Insurance if the employer pays less.

### Passengers

An employer can pay up to **5p a mile per passenger** to an employee who carries fellow employees in a car or van on a
journey that is also business travel for them. gov.uk says these payments are tax-free, and for an employee's own car or
van they aren't reported and carry no National Insurance. If the employer pays less than 5p, or nothing, there is no
relief to claim.

### Company cars

The approved mileage rates don't apply to a company vehicle. For business journeys in a **company car**, HMRC publishes
**advisory fuel rates** by engine size and fuel, which it reviews four times a year, on 1 March, 1 June, 1 September and
1 December. Paid at or below them, gov.uk says there is no taxable profit and no Class 1A National Insurance to pay.
The current figures are on gov.uk's "Advisory fuel rates" page.

- A **company electric car** uses HMRC's advisory electric rate, for home and for public charging: the guide to
  [the electric car mileage rate](/en/guides/electric-car-mileage-rate/) explains it.
- **Your own electric car** uses the ordinary car rate above, 55p then 25p for 2026 to 2027, as the same guide shows.
- In a company car, gov.uk says you can claim tax relief on what you spent on fuel or electricity for business trips
  that your employer didn't pay back, on the actual cost.

## The 10,000-mile threshold

- **It runs per tax year**, from 6 April to 5 April. The count starts again at zero each 6 April.
- **All cars and vans count together.** The Act adds up every business mile by car or van in the job, and in any
  "associated employment" (another job with the same employer, for example). A new car halfway through the year doesn't
  start a new 10,000.
- **Motorcycles and bicycles have no band**: one rate for every business mile, worked out separately from cars.

### Example: two cars in the 2026 to 2027 tax year

A fictional example of HMRC's method. An employee drives their own car 4,200 business miles between 6 April and
31 August 2026, then sells it and drives their new car 8,200 business miles up to 5 April 2027. They also cycle 300
business miles.

| Step | Amount |
| ---- | -----: |
| Business miles by car: 4,200 + 8,200 | 12,400 miles |
| First 10,000 miles × 55p | £5,500 |
| Next 2,400 miles × 25p | £600 |
| **Approved amount, cars and vans** | **£6,100** |
| Bicycle: 300 miles × 20p (worked out separately) | £60 |

For the car miles, two ways it could go:

- **The employer pays 40p a mile**: 12,400 × 40p = £4,960. That is £1,140 below the approved amount, so the employee
  could claim mileage allowance relief on £1,140.
- **The employer pays 55p for every mile**: 12,400 × 55p = £6,820. The £720 above the £6,100 approved amount is taxable,
  through a P11D or payroll. For National Insurance there is nothing above the limit, because National Insurance uses
  55p for every car mile.

## 2027 to 2028: what has been announced

The government's "Budget 2026 — Overview of tax legislation and rates" on gov.uk, published on 28 October 2026 and updated on 5 November
2026, says (section 2.7) that the government will raise the rate for business miles **above 10,000** from 25p to **35p a
mile**, for approved mileage allowance payments and for the self-employed flat rates, **from 6 April 2027**. It says the
change will be made by a statutory instrument "later in the year".

On 27 November 2026 that is an announcement, not law: no such statutory instrument was on legislation.gov.uk, and HMRC's
rates page still listed rates up to the 2026 to 2027 tax year. Before using a rate for a journey from 6 April 2027 on,
check HMRC's "Travel — mileage and fuel rates and allowances" page.

## What to check for your own case

This guide lists HMRC's rates and who uses them. It doesn't say what you are owed or what your employer must pay. A few
points depend on your situation:

- **Whose vehicle it is, and what your employer pays per mile.** Your employer's expenses policy or payroll team can tell
  you.
- **Which journeys count as business travel.** gov.uk says travel to and from your work doesn't count, unless it's a
  temporary place of work. For a journey of your own, ask HMRC or a tax adviser.
- **The tax year of each journey**, from 6 April: each tax year uses its own rates.
- **The rate on the day.** HMRC's rates page has the approved rates, and its "Advisory fuel rates" page the company car
  rates, which change every quarter.
