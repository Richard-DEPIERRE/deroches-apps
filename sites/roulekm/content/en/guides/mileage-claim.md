A **mileage claim** is how an employee who uses their own car, van, motorcycle or bicycle for work asks their employer
to pay for the business miles. Your employer sets its own rate and its own way of claiming (a form, a spreadsheet or an
expenses app, for example). The claim lists each business journey: the date, where it started and ended, the reason
and the miles.

For the **2026 to 2027 tax year** (6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027), HMRC lets an employer pay up to **55p a mile for the
first 10,000 business miles by car or van, then 25p a mile**, with no income tax or National Insurance and nothing to
report. If it pays more, the excess is taxable. If it pays less, or nothing, you can claim tax relief from HMRC on the
difference.

This guide is for employees. Self-employed? Sole traders claim business miles on their tax return instead: see
[how a self-employed mileage claim works](/en/guides/self-employed-mileage-claim/).

## What a mileage claim shows

Your employer's form or system decides the exact boxes. The details below are the ones HMRC's guidance relies on, so a
claim that has them can be checked journey by journey:

- **The date** of each business journey.
- **Where it started and where it ended.** When an employee claims tax relief, gov.uk asks for mileage logs with the
  postcode of the start and the end of every journey, so with the postcodes on it, one log can serve both.
- **The business reason** for the journey: the client, the site, the meeting.
- **The business miles**, not your usual commute (see below).
- **The vehicle**: car or van, motorcycle or bicycle, as each kind has its own rate. Your own electric or hybrid car
  counts as a car (see [the electric car mileage rate](/en/guides/electric-car-mileage-rate/)).
- **Passengers**, if your employer pays a passenger rate: HMRC lets it pay up to 5p a mile tax-free for each fellow
  employee you carry in your car or van on a journey that is also business travel for them.

HMRC's Employment Income Manual (EIM31390) says that even when an employer pays within the approved amount and reports
nothing, it still keeps records of the payments and of the business journeys they relate to.

### Example: one week's claim in November 2026

A fictional example, in the 2026 to 2027 tax year. An employee based at their employer's office in Leeds uses their own
car for three business journeys in one week. Their employer's policy pays 45p a mile.

| Date | From | To | Business reason | Miles |
| ---- | ---- | -- | --------------- | ----: |
| 2 November 2026 | Leeds office (LS1) | Client, York (YO1), and back | Quarterly review with the client | 50 |
| 4 November 2026 | Leeds office (LS1) | Training centre, Wakefield (WF1), and back | Product training | 20 |
| 6 November 2026 | Leeds office (LS1) | Supplier, Harrogate (HG1), and back | Delivery problem on site | 32 |
| **Total** | | | | **102** |

At the employer's 45p a mile, the claim is for 102 × 45p = **£45.90**. HMRC's approved amount for those miles is 102 ×
55p = £56.10 (all within the first 10,000 business miles of the tax year), so there is no tax on the £45.90. Over the
whole tax year, the gap between what the employer paid and the approved amount is what mileage allowance relief is
worked out on (see below).

## Business miles, not commuting

Only **business travel** counts. In the Income Tax (Earnings and Pensions) Act 2003 and HMRC's guide 490 ("Tax and
National Insurance contributions for employee travel"):

- **Ordinary commuting doesn't count**: travel between home and a **permanent workplace**. For most employees, HMRC says,
  that is the journey they make most days between home and their normal place of work.
- **Travel to a temporary workplace can count**: a place you go to for a task of limited duration or another temporary
  purpose. gov.uk says travel to and from work doesn't count "unless it's a temporary place of work".
- **The 24-month rule**: under section 339 of the Act, a workplace is not temporary when the continuous work there lasts,
  or is expected to last, more than 24 months. HMRC's guide counts the work there as continuous when the employee
  spends 40% or more of their working time at that place.

Depots, jobs defined by an area, working from home and agency work have their own rules, with worked examples, in
chapter 3 of HMRC's guide 490 (listed in the sources below).

## How much you get, and the tax side

**The rate is your employer's.** HMRC's manual (EIM31210) lists the forms a mileage allowance can take: a rate per
business mile paid on actual miles, a lump sum towards the business share of the car's standing costs, or a mix, for
example a monthly lump sum with a lower rate per mile and a separate fuel rate.

**HMRC's approved rates set how much is tax-free.** For 2026 to 2027 they are 55p a mile for the first 10,000 business
miles by car or van and 25p after that (24p for motorcycles, 20p for bicycles); every rate, past years included, is in
[HMRC mileage rates for every vehicle and case](/en/guides/hmrc-mileage-rates/). The **approved amount** is your business
miles in the tax year × the rate, with all your cars and vans counted together. gov.uk's own example: 12,000 business
miles by car comes to £6,000 (10,000 × 55p, plus 2,000 × 25p).

- **Paid at or below the approved amount**: gov.uk says the employer doesn't report it to HMRC and there's no tax on it.
- **Paid above it**: the excess is taxable. The employer either reports it on form P11D, or adds it to your pay and takes
  tax off through payroll.
- **National Insurance** works differently: the employer uses 55p for every business mile by car or van from 6 April
  2026, per pay period, with no 10,000-mile band. Anything paid above that counts as earnings for Class 1 National
  Insurance.

The government's Budget 2026 papers announce 35p a mile instead of 25p for business miles above 10,000 from 6 April
2027. On 28 November 2026 that was not law yet: the rates hub above has the detail.

## Fuel receipts and VAT

Some employers ask for fuel receipts with a mileage claim. HMRC's VAT rules explain why, and they concern the
employer's VAT, not your tax. VAT Notice 700/64 ("Motoring expenses") says that a VAT-registered business paying a
mileage allowance can reclaim VAT on the **fuel part** of the allowance, for fuel used for its taxable business:

- the allowance must be based on miles actually driven, and the VAT is the fuel part × the VAT fraction;
- the fuel cost per mile can come from HMRC's advisory fuel rates or from rates published by motoring organisations
  such as the AA or the RAC;
- the employer keeps **VAT invoices for the fuel** (full or less detailed), covering at least the business fuel, and an
  invoice dated after the period claimed can't support the claim. In practice, the notice says, it may be advisable for
  employers to have their employees keep all their fuel receipts.

Whether you must hand in receipts is your employer's rule. For your own tax, it changes nothing: gov.uk says the approved
mileage rate covers the cost of owning and running your vehicle, fuel included, and HMRC's advisory fuel rates are for
company cars, not your own.

## If your employer pays less, or nothing

If what your employer pays for a kind of vehicle over the tax year adds up to less than the approved amount, or it pays
nothing at all, section 231 of the Act gives you **mileage allowance relief** on the difference: income tax relief,
claimed from HMRC, not from your employer. There's none for journeys as a passenger, in a company vehicle, for National
Insurance, or for passenger payments below 5p. How it's worked out, how to claim it and how far back are in
[mileage allowance relief for employees](/en/guides/mileage-allowance-relief/).

## Deadlines and records

- **The deadline for a claim to your employer is set by its own policy**: check your expenses policy or ask payroll. HMRC's manual (EIM31230) compares the approved amount with what was paid for the tax year's miles, even when
  the payment comes shortly after the tax year ends for administrative reasons.
- **What to keep.** HMRC's guide 490 (chapter 12) lists, for employees: the details of claims you made to your employer
  and the receipts behind them (unless your employer holds them), your own mileage records, and the P11D details of any
  expenses payments.
- **How long.** The same chapter says that an employee who claims tax relief keeps the records for at least 22 months
  after the end of the tax year they apply to. The relief guide linked above covers the claim itself.

## What to check for your own case

This guide explains how a mileage claim and HMRC's rates work. It doesn't say what you are owed or what your employer
must pay. A few points depend on your situation:

- **Your employer's expenses policy**: its rate per mile, the form, the deadline, whether it wants fuel receipts and
  whether it pays a passenger rate. Payroll or HR can tell you.
- **Whether a journey is business travel**, especially to a site you visit often or for a long project. HMRC's guide 490
  has worked examples; for a journey of your own, ask HMRC or a tax adviser.
- **The tax year of each journey**, from 6 April: each tax year uses its own rates.
- **What you were paid over the year.** If it is below the approved amount, HMRC's "Claim tax relief for your job
  expenses" pages on gov.uk say how to claim relief.
