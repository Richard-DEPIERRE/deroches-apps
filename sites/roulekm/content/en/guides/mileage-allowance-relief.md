**Mileage allowance relief** (MAR) is income tax relief for UK employees who use their own car, van, motorcycle or
bicycle for business travel and whose employer pays them less than HMRC's approved mileage rate, or nothing at all. The
relief is on the difference between the **approved amount** (business miles × the approved rate) and what the employer
paid for those miles. For cars and vans the approved rate is **55p a mile for the first 10,000 business miles from the
2026 to 2027 tax year** (45p up to 5 April 2026), then 25p a mile. It is claimed from HMRC, online or on form P87, or on
a Self Assessment return, for the current tax year and the 4 before it. The rates, a worked example, what counts as
business travel and how to claim are below.

## Who it's for

Section 231 of the Income Tax (Earnings and Pensions) Act 2003 gives an employee mileage allowance relief for a tax year
when both of these apply:

- they use their own car, van, motorcycle or bicycle for **business travel**, and
- the mileage allowance payments their employer made for that kind of vehicle in the tax year add up to **less than
  the approved amount**.

There is no relief for a journey made as a passenger, or in a company vehicle. The approved rate is meant to cover the
whole cost of owning and running the vehicle: gov.uk says fuel, electricity, vehicle tax, MOTs and repairs can't be
claimed separately, and HMRC's Employment Income Manual (EIM31330) says actual costs are not an alternative.

## HMRC's approved mileage rates, by tax year

A tax year runs from 6 April to 5 April. These are the rates in section 230 of the Act and on HMRC's rates page:

| Vehicle | 2026 to 2027 tax year (from 6 April 2026) | 2011 to 2012 up to 2025 to 2026 |
| ------- | ----------------------------------------: | ------------------------------: |
| Car or van, first 10,000 business miles in the tax year | 55p a mile | 45p a mile |
| Car or van, each business mile over 10,000 | 25p a mile | 25p a mile |
| Motorcycle | 24p a mile | 24p a mile |
| Bicycle | 20p a mile | 20p a mile |

- The 55p comes from section 2 of the Taxation (Energy and Vehicles) Act 2026, which applies it to the 2026 to 2027 tax
  year and later years.
- **The 10,000 miles are counted per tax year**, adding up all business miles by car or van in that job and in any
  "associated employment" as the Act defines it (another job with the same employer, for example).
- Each kind of vehicle is worked out separately. An electrically assisted pedal bike uses the bicycle rate; any other
  electric bike uses the motorcycle rate.

These are the rates HMRC published, checked on 14 November 2026. For the 2027 to 2028 tax year, check HMRC's
"Travel — mileage and fuel rates and allowances" page.

## How the relief is worked out

HMRC's manual works it out like this, separately for each kind of vehicle:

1. Add up the **business miles** for the tax year.
2. Work out the **approved amount**: the miles × the rate (for cars and vans, the first 10,000 miles at the higher rate,
   the rest at 25p).
3. Take away what the **employer paid** for those miles. If something is left, that is the amount of mileage allowance
   relief. If the employer paid more than the approved amount, there is no relief, and the excess is taxable.

The relief is a deduction from your earnings, so what it is worth in tax depends on the rate of income tax you pay.
gov.uk's own example: £60 of relief at a 20% tax rate is worth £12. You must have paid tax in that year, and the relief
can't be worth more than the tax you paid.

### Worked example: the 2025 to 2026 tax year

A fictional example, to show HMRC's method. An employee drove their own car 11,200 business miles between 6 April 2025
and 5 April 2026, and their employer paid 30p a mile.

| Step | Amount |
| ---- | -----: |
| First 10,000 business miles × 45p | £4,500 |
| Next 1,200 business miles × 25p | £300 |
| **Approved amount** | **£4,800** |
| Employer paid: 11,200 miles × 30p | £3,360 |
| **Amount the relief is on** (£4,800 − £3,360) | **£1,440** |

At the basic rate of income tax for 2025 to 2026 in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, 20%, relief on £1,440 is worth
£288 in tax (20% × £1,440). Someone who pays tax at another rate, lives in Scotland (where the bands and rates differ),
or paid less tax than that in the year would get a different figure.

The same driving and the same 30p a mile in the 2026 to 2027 tax year would give an approved amount of £5,800 (10,000 ×
55p, plus 1,200 × 25p) and relief on £2,440. If an employer pays nothing at all, the relief is on the whole approved
amount.

## What counts as business travel, and what doesn't

The Act ties "business travel" to the journeys an employee could deduct as travel expenses: travel in the course of the
job, or to a place they have to attend to do the job. Two kinds of journey are left out, in HMRC's words (guide 490,
chapter 3):

- **Ordinary commuting**: "any travel between a permanent workplace and" home, or any other place that isn't a
  workplace. For most employees, that's the journey they make most days between home and their normal place of work.
- **Private travel**: a journey between home and a place they don't have to be for work, or between two such places.

A **temporary workplace** is different: gov.uk says travel to and from work doesn't count "unless it's a temporary place
of work". Under the Act's 24-month rule, a workplace is not temporary when the work there lasts, or is expected to
last, more than 24 months; HMRC's guide applies it when the employee spends, or is likely to spend, 40% or more of
their working time there over that period.

Depots, jobs covering an area, working from home, agency work and more than one workplace each have their own rules:
chapter 3 of HMRC's guide 490, "Tax and National Insurance contributions for employee travel", covers them.

## The records to keep

gov.uk asks you to keep records of the **dates and mileage of your work journeys**, and a claim made online or by post
goes in with copies of the mileage log. HMRC says each log must show:

- **the reason for every journey**;
- **the postcode where every journey started**;
- **the postcode where every journey ended**.

If you have more than one job, HMRC asks for a separate log for each. A claim also says which job the miles were for and
whether the employer paid anything towards them, with evidence of how much.

On how long to keep them: HMRC's guide 490 (chapter 12) says employees who claim tax relief keep their records for **at
least 22 months after the end of the tax year** they apply to. gov.uk's page on keeping records says the same for a
Self Assessment return sent on time, and at least 15 months after sending a return sent late. The same page says there
are no rules on how records are kept: on paper, digitally or in software. HMRC adds that employees must be able to back
up what they claim, and that it recovers relief that was overstated, with interest and, where appropriate, penalties.

If you'd rather keep the log on your phone, Roulekm (coming soon to iPhone and Android) keeps each business trip with its
date, where from and where to (you can type the postcodes HMRC asks for), the purpose and the miles. It also applies
HMRC's approved rates for each kind of vehicle over the tax year, 10,000-mile band included.

## How to claim, and how far back

gov.uk's "Claim tax relief for your job expenses" guide, read on 14 November 2026, gives these routes:

- **If you fill in a Self Assessment tax return**, the claim goes on the return instead.
- **Online.** The guide's "Vehicles you use for work" page has a Start now button to an HMRC service that checks whether
  you can claim and how. HMRC's briefing says claims and their evidence can be sent through an online form since 23
  December 2024.
- **By post, on form P87.** P87 is HMRC's claim form for job expenses, mileage included, and the only form it accepts
  by post. A postal claim must be made within 4 years from the end of the tax year, the total expenses claimed for each
  tax year must be **£2,500 or less** (above that, a Self Assessment return is needed), and tax must have been paid in
  that year.

For the current tax year, HMRC usually changes the tax code. For earlier years, it either changes the tax code or pays a
refund.

**How far back.** gov.uk says claims can cover the current tax year and the 4 previous tax years. The Taxes Management
Act 1970 (section 43) sets the limit at 4 years after the end of the tax year. On 15 November 2026 that means:

| Tax year | Dates | Last day to claim |
| -------- | ----- | ----------------- |
| 2022 to 2023 | 6 April 2022 – 5 April 2023 | 5 April 2027 |
| 2023 to 2024 | 6 April 2023 – 5 April 2024 | 5 April 2028 |
| 2024 to 2025 | 6 April 2024 – 5 April 2025 | 5 April 2029 |
| 2025 to 2026 | 6 April 2025 – 5 April 2026 | 5 April 2030 |
| 2026 to 2027 (current) | 6 April 2026 – 5 April 2027 | 5 April 2031 |

Each claim uses the approved rates of its own tax year: 45p for the first 10,000 car or van miles up to 2025 to 2026,
55p from 2026 to 2027.

## What to check with HMRC for your own case

This guide explains the rule and HMRC's method. It doesn't say whether you are owed relief, or how much. A few points
depend on your own situation:

- **Whether a journey is business travel.** HMRC's guide 490 has worked examples for the edge cases; for a journey of
  your own, ask HMRC or a tax adviser.
- **What your employer paid, and for which miles.** Ask your employer for the yearly figure for your own vehicle.
- **The tax you paid and your rate.** The "Check your Income Tax" service on gov.uk shows your tax code and the tax
  you've paid in the current tax year.
- **Other vehicles and other work.** This relief is for employees using their own vehicle. Company cars and vans, and
  driving for your own business if you're self-employed, follow other rules, which gov.uk explains separately.
